\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) summarise research papers fluently, but the facts they report
come without evidence, and in research an invented number is worse than none. We present
PaperLens, a system that turns a paper into a structured record in which every item carries
the verbatim sentence it came from; deterministic code locates that sentence in the PDF,
highlights it, and checks that it supports the item. On top of this grounded extraction,
PaperLens scores reproducibility against a 21-item checklist, verifies numeric claims
against the paper's own results tables, and compares the paper's hyperparameters with its
released code by static analysis. One rule governs the design: \emph{the LLM extracts and
Python decides}, so every score, difference and verdict is computed deterministically,
and the whole system runs on the free tier of a commercial LLM API. On a new gold set of
50 papers from nine machine-learning areas (2{,}796 items), extraction reaches a core-field
F1 of 0.60 with precision of 0.81--0.94 per field; 90\% of extracted items are verified
against the paper and 1\% are hallucinated. A manual audit of all extracted items absent
from the gold set finds only 4\% wrong, showing that the scores are lower bounds. Requiring
a located, supporting quote drives hallucinations to zero at a moderate cost in recall,
and recall on appendix hyperparameters is the main open problem.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
scientific information extraction, attribution, grounding, reproducibility, large language
models, claim verification

\section{Introduction}

Much of the time spent reading a paper goes into finding the same facts: datasets,
baselines, metrics, hyperparameters and compute. Judging whether the work can be reproduced
is harder still. No paper in a survey of AI research documented every factor needed for
reproduction~\cite{gundersen2018state}; independent re-implementation of 255 papers
succeeded only about two times in three~\cite{raff2019step}; and released code often uses
settings other than those reported~\cite{pineau2021improving,collberg2016repeatability}.

LLMs are an obvious aid, but their output is unattributed and they hallucinate
facts~\cite{ji2023hallucination}. Attribution research shows that generated statements must
be checked against an identified source to be trusted~\cite{rashkin2023ais,gao2023alce}.
Where a wrong learning rate silently ruins a replication, unverifiable output is of little
value.

PaperLens is built around one principle: \textbf{the LLM extracts, and Python decides}. The
LLM only reads text and returns schema-validated items, each with a verbatim quote.
Deterministic code does everything else: it locates every quote in the PDF, scores
reproducibility, matches claims to table cells and recomputes their deltas, compares the
paper with its code, and issues every verdict. Because the system runs on the free tier of
the Gemini API~\cite{gemini2023}, each design choice also minimises LLM calls (about six
per paper). Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A grounded extraction pipeline in which every item carries evidence that is located
  in the PDF and checked for support; unverifiable items are labelled, never dropped.
\item Three deterministic checks on top of it: a reproducibility scorecard, a claim
  verifier that recomputes claimed differences from parsed tables, and a static
  paper-versus-code comparison that never executes repository code.
\item A 50-paper, 2{,}796-item gold set across nine ML areas with a documented matching
  scheme, a manual audit of unmatched predictions, and LLM-free ablations of evidence
  policy, parser and retrieval.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Scientific information extraction.} SciERC~\cite{luan2018sciierc} and
SciREX~\cite{jain2020scirex} annotate entities and task--dataset--metric relations; Hou et
al.~\cite{hou2019tdms} and AxCell~\cite{kardas2020axcell} extract leaderboard results. These
systems rely on trained encoders such as SciBERT~\cite{beltagy2019scibert}, and Dagdelen et
al.\ fine-tune LLMs for structured extraction~\cite{dagdelen2024structured}. PaperLens uses
a general LLM without fine-tuning and makes evidence a checked, first-class output.

\textbf{Parsing.} GROBID converts PDFs to structured TEI with
coordinates~\cite{lopez2009grobid} and underlies S2ORC~\cite{lo2020s2orc}; Nougat and
Docling offer vision-based alternatives~\cite{blecher2024nougat,auer2024docling}, and table
recognition has dedicated benchmarks~\cite{zhong2020pubtabnet,smock2022pubtables}.

\textbf{Attribution and question answering.} RAG conditions generation on retrieved
passages~\cite{lewis2020rag}; ALCE and AIS evaluate whether statements are attributable to
sources~\cite{gao2023alce,rashkin2023ais}; FActScore and SelfCheckGPT detect unsupported
facts~\cite{min2023factscore,manakul2023selfcheckgpt}. Qasper anchors questions in
papers~\cite{dasigi2021qasper}, PaperQA and OpenScholar answer with citations over large
collections~\cite{lala2023paperqa,asai2026openscholar}, and ScholarPhi and the Semantic
Reader ground definitions inside the reading
interface~\cite{head2021scholarphi,lo2024semanticreader}.

\textbf{Verification, reproducibility and reviewing.} SciFact, TabFact and SciTab verify
claims against abstracts and tables, and show that table reasoning remains hard for
LLMs~\cite{wadden2020scifact,chen2020tabfact,lu2023scitab}. SUPER, CORE-Bench and PaperBench
ask agents to run or replicate research
code~\cite{bogin2024super,siegel2024corebench,starace2025paperbench}, at hours of compute per
paper. LLM reviewers overlap with human reviewers in coverage but are unreliable for
scoring~\cite{liang2024feedback,zhou2024reliable}.

\textbf{Positioning.} Table~\ref{tab:related} compares design properties. Each line of work
covers part of the problem; PaperLens is, to our knowledge, the only one that fills a full
extraction schema including hyperparameters, checks a quote for every item in code, leaves
numeric verdicts to deterministic code, and adds reproducibility, claim and code checks
without fine-tuning or code execution. The comparison is of design, not accuracy: the
systems address different tasks, so their scores are not comparable.

\section{System Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture. A web interface accepts a PDF or a link (arXiv,
DOI, OpenReview, ACL Anthology); a worker runs the pipeline and streams progress. Every LLM
call passes through one gateway that caches responses, rate-limits per model, retries with
backoff, falls back to a backup model when one is overloaded, validates the output against a
schema with one repair attempt, and logs the model and prompt version. Results are keyed by
(paper, stage, prompt version, model), so stages are idempotent and cached re-runs are free.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Parsing}\label{sec:parse}
GROBID supplies sections, sentences with coordinates, references and resolved in-text
citations; all coordinates are kept in PDF points so any span can later be highlighted.
Because GROBID's table grids were often wrong or misplaced, PaperLens locates each table by
its own caption (``Table~3:'') and rebuilds the grid from the PDF's word positions, merging
wrapped cells and parsing values such as $24.3\pm0.2$. A grid that fails a sanity check is
dropped: no grid is better than a wrong one.

\subsection{Grounded extraction}
Sections are grouped into front matter, method, experiments and closing, and each group is
sent in one schema-constrained call with its paragraphs labelled by chunk id. For each item
the LLM returns a value, a verbatim quote and the chunk id. Python then locates each quote
$q$ in the cited chunk $c$ and, failing that, in the whole paper $D$, using the
Levenshtein-based partial-ratio similarity $s$~\cite{levenshtein1966} of normalised strings:
\begin{equation}
  \hat b(q) = \arg\max_{b \in \{c\} \cup D} s(q, b), \qquad
  \text{located} \iff s\bigl(q,\hat b(q)\bigr) \geq \tau ,
\end{equation}
with $\tau = 90$. Located quotes are mapped to sentence boxes; a quoted table row
highlights that row. One batched call then labels each located quote as
\emph{verified}, \emph{partial} or \emph{unsupported}; an unfound quote is
\emph{unlocated}. Such items are kept and badged, so users see them and the evaluation
counts them. Code links are found by rules, not by the LLM.

\subsection{Reproducibility scorecard}
A 21-item checklist derived from the NeurIPS checklist~\cite{pineau2021improving} is split
into five groups $g$ with weights $W_g$: code ($0.25$), data ($0.20$), experimental setup
($0.25$), compute ($0.10$) and statistical reporting ($0.20$). Rules (regular expressions,
document structure, repository metadata) decide items first; one LLM call judges the rest,
and each judgement must quote the paper. An LLM ``present'' whose quote is not confirmed
is capped at ``partial''. With $p(\text{present})=1$, $p(\text{partial})=0.5$,
$p(\text{missing})=0$ and item weights $w_i$ over the scored items $S_g$,
\begin{equation}
  \text{score} = 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_g W_g\, \sigma_g}{\sum_g W_g}, \qquad
  \sigma_g = \frac{\sum_{i\in S_g} w_i\, p(s_i)}{\sum_{i\in S_g} w_i}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Claim verification}
For each quantitative claim the LLM only copies the method, baseline, metric, dataset and
claimed difference $\delta^{*}$ as written. Python finds the candidate cells $x_m$ and $x_b$
in the parsed results tables, recomputes $\hat\delta = x_m - x_b$ (or a ratio), and labels
the claim \emph{supported} if $|\hat\delta - \delta^{*}| \leq \epsilon$ ($\epsilon=0.15$).
\emph{Contradicted} requires exactly one unambiguous pair of cells; ambiguous or missing
matches yield \emph{unsupported}, so a parsing error cannot make a paper look wrong.

\subsection{Paper versus code}
Repository links are ranked from the paper text, separating the authors' own code from
libraries the work is ``based on''. The repository is cloned shallowly with hooks, symlinks
and local protocols disabled and a size cap, and a parse-only scanner reads Python ASTs,
YAML, JSON, TOML and README flags inside a sandbox; repository code is never executed.
Names are aligned by a synonym table ($\texttt{lr} \leftrightarrow
\texttt{learning\_rate}$) and values compared after normalisation ($3\mathrm{e}{-4} =
0.0003$).

\subsection{Chat, notation and review}
Chat retrieves paragraphs with BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25} and dense
embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sbert,karpukhin2020dpr} fused by reciprocal rank
fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf}; Python removes citation markers the model invents and refuses
uncited answers, or answers nothing when retrieval finds nothing relevant. A notation
glossary is built from the arXiv LaTeX source (read, never compiled), with each symbol's
meaning grounded in a sentence; key references are those cited most often in the text,
optionally enriched by Semantic Scholar~\cite{valenzuela2015meaningful}. A draft review
lists quoted strengths, weaknesses and questions, keeps facts computed in Python separate,
and gives no score.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Gold set.} We annotated 50 arXiv papers from 2014--2024 (Table~\ref{tab:gold}).
Ten \emph{development} papers were used while building the system: Bahdanau et
al.~\cite{bahdanau2015}, VGG~\cite{simonyan2015vgg}, ResNet~\cite{he2016resnet},
Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert},
RoBERTa~\cite{liu2019roberta}, DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert},
SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simclr}, ViT~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} and LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora}.
Forty \emph{held-out} papers were added afterwards, balanced over nine areas and varied in
layout: appendix hyperparameter tables, prompting papers with few settings, theory-heavy
papers and papers without released code. The 2{,}796 gold items cover task, datasets,
baselines, metrics, hyperparameters, compute and code URLs, defined in an annotation guide.
The reference annotations were produced by an LLM (Claude) reading each paper's full text
under the guide, then adjudicated once against PaperLens' output: 48 omitted items that the
papers state were added, no alias was written in the system's wording, and nothing was
removed. Every change is logged.

\textbf{Matching and metrics.} Text values are normalised (Unicode NFKD, number words to
digits). A prediction matches a gold item when the two are nearly equal (fuzzy ratio
$\geq 88$ with equal digit sequences, so CIFAR-10 never matches CIFAR-100), or when one
contains the other and the remainder is filler. Hyperparameters must agree on the name
(through a synonym table) and on the parsed value ($6\% = 0.06$). For predicted items $P$
and gold items $G$ of a field,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{P} = \frac{|\{p\in P : \exists g,\, p \sim g\}|}{|P|}, \quad
  \mathrm{R} = \frac{|\{g\in G : \exists p,\, p \sim g\}|}{|G|},
\end{equation}
and F1 is their harmonic mean, micro-averaged over papers; the \emph{core-field F1} is the
mean over task, datasets, baselines, metrics and hyperparameters. The \emph{hallucination
rate} is the share of extracted items that are unlocated, unsupported or without evidence;
\emph{numeric accuracy} is the share of extracted hyperparameters with a gold name whose
value is correct; \emph{hyperparameter coverage} is the share of gold (paper, name) pairs
with a correct value extracted.

\textbf{Configuration.} Extraction uses Gemini Flash-Lite on the free tier with GROBID 0.8.1
and the thresholds above; all numbers come from a single run over the 50 papers, and all
tables and figures are regenerated by one command from the stored outputs.

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports per-field scores. Precision on the core fields is
0.81--0.94; recall is the bottleneck, above all for hyperparameters (0.27) and tasks
(0.37), giving a core-field F1 of 0.60. Of the extracted hyperparameters whose name
matched a gold name, 91\% carried the right value. 90\% of all 2{,}283 extracted items are
verified against the paper, and 1\% are unlocated or unsupported.

\subsection{Evidence policy (ablation A3)}
Because every item carries an evidence status, the same extraction can be scored under
stricter policies without new LLM calls (Fig.~\ref{fig:a3}). On the development set with an
earlier pipeline version, which left more quotes unlocated, requiring a located quote cut
the hallucination rate from 10\% to 2\%, and additionally requiring support cut it to 0\%,
with precision unchanged (0.96--0.97) and recall falling from 0.54 to 0.43. On the 50-paper
run nearly every quote is located, so the strictest policy only removes the last 1\% at a
recall cost of 0.03. We therefore keep every item in the interface with its status badge
rather than filtering.

\subsection{Parser (A1) and retrieval (A6)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:a1} measures how many gold values survive parsing at all, an upper bound on
recall. Raw PyMuPDF text keeps more values than GROBID (90\% vs 83\%), mostly numbers in
tables, footnotes and appendix lists, but GROBID recovers the structure the pipeline
depends on (1{,}520 vs 1{,}165 section headings, 3{,}026 vs 2{,}394 references, 580 vs 243
equations). For hyperparameters the gap is widest: 33\% of gold values never reach the
extractor through GROBID's text. With a ceiling of 67\%, the observed 30\% coverage means the
extractor recovers less than half of what the parse makes available. For chat, recall@5 on
2{,}086 question--item pairs is 45\% for BM25, 36\% for dense retrieval and 45\% for the
hybrid, which helps on datasets, metrics and hyperparameters but loses on task questions
(45\% vs 53\%); keeping BM25 in the fusion is therefore essential.

\subsection{Downstream checks}
On the development set, the reproducibility scorecard agreed with human annotations on 158
of 210 checklist decisions (75\%, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.57$~\cite{cohen1960kappa}); the main
disagreements concern training time and re-run baselines. Repository discovery found the
official code for all eight development papers that release it. As an example of the code
check, BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert} reports pre-training with learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch
size 256 and 1M steps, while the defaults of the official \texttt{run\_pretraining.py} are
$5\times10^{-5}$, 32 and 100{,}000; PaperLens reports the mismatch with the paper's
sentence beside the code location. The defaults are legitimate command-line values, but
running the script as is would not reproduce the paper.

\section{Error Analysis}\label{sec:errors}

\textbf{Unmatched predictions.} We read each of the 246 extracted items of the held-out
papers that matched no gold item against the paper (Table~\ref{tab:audit}). Only 10 (4\%)
are wrong: model families listed as datasets, a method listed as its own baseline, two
metrics and two values the paper does not state, and one code link absent from the text.
Half (127) are correct items the strict matcher misses, either through hyperparameter
names (``lambda'' vs \texttt{loss\_weight\_lambda}) or through values garbled by
superscripts in the PDF text layer (``3.0e\^{}$-$\^{}4'', ``2 24'' for $2^{24}$). A
further 31 were gold omissions, since added, and 78 are out of scope, mostly third-party
links and compute descriptions without hardware or time. Precision in Table~\ref{tab:main}
is thus a lower bound.

\textbf{Missed gold items.} The run missed 1{,}602 of 2{,}796 gold items, 956 of them
hyperparameters. Four causes recur. (i)~\emph{Per-size appendix settings}: LLaMA, Whisper
and T5 list dimensions, heads, layers and learning rates for every model size, and the
extraction usually returns one size. (ii)~\emph{Narrow tasks}: the model names one or two
tasks where the experiments address several (e.g.\ SAM's edge detection and object
proposals). (iii)~\emph{Long benchmark suites}: DINOv2 misses 29 of 42 datasets and Whisper
17 of 21, while LLaMA, whose benchmarks appear in running text, misses 4 of 27.
(iv)~\emph{Parsing}: 17\% of all gold values and 33\% of hyperparameter values are absent
from GROBID's text (A1).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Precision by construction, recall by effort.} Requiring a quote for every item and
checking it in code yields high precision and a hallucination rate that can be driven to
zero. Recall instead depends on the model reading every table and appendix, and on the
parser keeping them; table-aware extraction of per-size settings and a parse that retains
appendix tables are the most promising next steps.

\textbf{Deterministic decisions.} Scores, differences, verdicts and code comparisons are
computed in Python, so they are reproducible, unit-tested and conservative: an ambiguous
table match yields ``unsupported'', never ``contradicted''.

\textbf{Limitations and threats to validity.} The gold set has a single annotator, an LLM,
adjudicated once against this system's output, and the audit was done the same way; where
the annotating LLM and Gemini share blind spots, recall may be overestimated, while the
strict matcher underestimates precision. Areas outside NLP, CV and ML have one to three
papers. All results come from one run of one model on well-known papers that the model
may have seen in training. The scorecard's agreement with human judgements is below our
target of 80\%, and ablations that require additional LLM runs (extraction granularity,
model choice, extraction without quotes) are left to future work.

\textbf{Ethics.} Paper text is sent to a free-tier API whose inputs may be used by the
provider, so the interface warns against uploading unpublished work. Repository code is
never executed, and the draft review gives no score, consistent with evidence that LLM
reviews should support rather than replace human reviewers~\cite{liang2024feedback}.

\section{Conclusion}
PaperLens shows that a free-tier LLM can support careful paper reading when it is asked
only to extract and quote, while deterministic code locates every quote and makes every
decision. On 50 papers from nine areas it extracts with high precision (4\% of unmatched
predictions are wrong), 91\% numeric accuracy and 1\% hallucinated items, each item
traceable to a highlighted sentence. Closing the recall gap on appendix tables, and adding
independent human annotation, are the next steps.
